Everyday English: conversations, choices, and practical details

At the market, Nora asks the seller whether the pears are ready to eat. Some are soft enough for today; others will ripen on the kitchen shelf. She buys four pears, a loaf of bread, and a small bag of lentils. Before leaving, she checks the receipt. The total is correct, but she has forgotten her reusable bag. The seller offers a paper one, and she resolves to keep a folded cloth bag in her coat pocket.

“Could you tell me where the library is?”
“Walk past the bakery, turn left at the traffic lights, and continue until you reach the public garden. The entrance is opposite the fountain.”
“Is it within walking distance?”
“Yes, it should take about ten minutes at an ordinary pace.”

Milo is planning a meal with friends. One guest prefers mild food, another avoids dairy products, and a third is arriving late. He chooses a vegetable stew with rice and serves grated cheese separately. While the onions soften, he rinses the lentils, washes the tomatoes, and measures the water. He writes the arrival time on a note beside the door. A plan does not remove every uncertainty, but it makes the evening easier to organize.

The morning train is delayed. The departure board says twelve minutes, although the announcement warns that the estimate may change. A passenger opens the station map and looks for a sheltered waiting area. She sends a short message to her colleague: “My train is running late. I expect to arrive after nine. Please begin without me if necessary.” She does not claim to know the exact arrival time when the railway has not confirmed it.

Yesterday I walked to work. Today I am working from home. Tomorrow I will visit the office again. I have worked with this team for several months, and I had already met two of its members before I joined. If the weather stays dry, we may eat lunch outside. If it had rained yesterday, our outdoor meeting would have moved indoors.

Here are three ways to make the same request. “Open the window” is direct. “Please open the window” adds politeness. “Would you mind opening the window?” is a more indirect question. The appropriate wording depends on the setting, the relationship, and the urgency. Being polite does not require making a simple message difficult to understand.

The apartment has a narrow hallway, two bright bedrooms, and a kitchen facing the courtyard. A cupboard holds cups, plates, bowls, and a chipped blue teapot. The washing machine hums near the back door. Someone has left a scarf on the chair, but nobody remembers whose it is. These small details make the room feel occupied rather than merely furnished.

“I thought the appointment was on Thursday.”
“It was, but the office moved it to Friday afternoon.”
“Thanks for explaining. I had not seen the updated message.”
“No problem. I will forward the date and address.”

Some words sound similar but have different meanings. A quiet room has little noise; a quite unusual room is rather unusual. To borrow is to receive something temporarily, while to lend is to give it temporarily. Their notebook belongs to them; there is a notebook on the table; they’re looking for it. Context helps a reader decide which meaning fits.
